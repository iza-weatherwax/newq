# Voice

The house style. Every scene is written to this. It starts generic and gets
sharper every time the author says "this is good" or "no, not like that" —
see **Keepers** and **Misses** at the bottom, which outrank everything above them.

## Core

- Lean. If a sentence can go, it goes.
- Most of the page is dialogue and the main character's head. Narration is the glue, not the meal.
- Start the scene late (already in motion), leave early (before it's wrapped up).
- Trust the reader. Never explain what a line of dialogue already made clear.

## Dialogue

- People talk past each other, interrupt, answer the wrong question, and dodge.
- Tags: "said" or nothing. Action beats only when they add something.
- Every character wants something in the scene, even if it's a sandwich.
- No speeches. If someone talks for more than four lines, someone else should cut them off, or the speaker should notice they're rambling.

## Inner voice

- The main character's thoughts are a second, louder conversation running under the spoken one.
- Thoughts contradict what the character says out loud. That gap is where most of the comedy lives.
- Free indirect style: slip into the character's thoughts without "she thought" when the voice makes it obvious.
- Thoughts can be petty, wrong, and self-serving. That's what makes them honest.

## Description

- Only when it does a job: it gets a laugh, reveals character, or matters later.
- One precise, odd detail beats three general ones.
- Description filtered through the character. We see what *they* notice, judged the way *they* would judge it.

## Worldbuilding through people

- The world shows up in how people treat it as normal: complaints, habits, jokes, bureaucracy, gossip.
- Nobody explains the world to someone who already lives in it.
- If a fact is needed, a side character mentions it while wanting something else.
- It's fine for the reader to not fully understand yet. Confusion that feels lived-in is good confusion.

## Comedy

- Ironic, a bit absurd. The absurd is played completely straight by the characters.
- Humor comes from character (what they want, what they notice, what they refuse to admit), not from gags pasted on top.
- Never explain the joke. Never have a character laugh to cue the reader.
- Callbacks are gold. Reuse earlier details at the worst possible moment.

## Turning heavy

When the scene needs weight, the voice changes gear, not personality:

- Sentences get shorter. The inner voice gets quieter or stops trying to be funny.
- One small concrete detail carries the emotion. No naming the feeling.
- A joke can still happen, but it lands wrong, or the character notices it didn't help.
- Don't linger. Get out of the heavy moment a beat before it feels finished.

## Banned

- Opening with weather, waking up, or a paragraph of setting.
- Characters explaining how the world works to each other ("As you know…").
- Explaining the joke or the subtext.
- Purple prose, stacked adjectives, "a symphony of…", "a testament to…".
- Summarizing a character's feelings at the end of a scene.
- Ending on a neat moral or a reflective one-liner that ties everything up.
