# Unstick: doors, not homework

Being stuck feels like needing a plan. Don't give them one. Give them
something to react to. Choosing between concrete options is easy; answering
questions about motivation and structure is how a gardener loses the idea.

## 1. Read the garden

Read `voice.md`, `ledger.md`, and the last one or two scenes in `scenes/`.
The ledger's **Open threads** and **Running jokes** are your best material:
the answer is often something the story already planted and forgot.

## 2. Diagnose silently

Work out which kind of stuck this is from what they said and the latest scene.
Don't ask them to diagnose it. If you can't tell, assume "what happens next".

| Kind of stuck | Sign | Move |
|---|---|---|
| **What happens next** | Scene ended, no idea where to go | Three very different next beats |
| **Flat scene** | "This is boring", scene treading water | Someone walks in wanting something; a secret comes out early; the absurd thing gets worse |
| **Lost interest** | "I don't care anymore", long gap | Go back to the original beat; find what was exciting about it and aim straight at it, or jump ahead to a scene they *would* want to write |
| **Logic hole** | "This doesn't make sense" | A character notices the hole and complains about it; the complaint becomes a joke or a thread. Don't fix it with exposition |
| **Too many options** | "It could go anywhere" | Pick the smallest, most personal option and make it the only one |

## 3. Offer doors

Give **three doors**, never more. Each one is:

- a bold name of 2–4 words,
- one sentence of what happens,
- the first line of prose, in voice, so they can feel it.

Make them different in kind, not just in detail:

- one that pulls an **open thread** or callback from the ledger,
- one that is **absurd**: the strangest thing this world would treat as normal,
- one that is **heavy** or personal, if the story can take it; otherwise a
  complete left turn (jump in time, change point of view, new place).

End with one line: *"Pick one, mash two together, or say 'write the obvious' and
I'll write the obvious next bit so you can hate it."*

That last option is real: a quick, plain draft of the most predictable next
moment (~200 words) often shows the writer what they *don't* want, which is
enough to move.

## 4. Write it

When they pick (or mash, or twist), write the scene exactly as the `seed`
skill does: follow `voice.md`, save to `scenes/`, update `ledger.md`, and add
the short garden notes.

## Never

- Ask about motivation, theme, arc, stakes, or "what does your character want?".
- Suggest outlining, a beat sheet, or worldbuilding exercises.
- Give more than three doors, or a list of generic writing tips.
- Explain why they're stuck. Just open the doors.
