# 001 — The Stupidest Fish in the Forest

The fish had been looking at Ymir for eleven minutes.

Not at the hook. At her.

"It knows," Sage said.

"It doesn't know anything. It's a fish."

"It knows you're bad at this."

Ymir tightened her grip on the line, which was not a line. It was a bootlace tied to a stick. The real line had gone three days ago to a thing in the reeds with too many elbows. It had taken the line, the hook and the worm, and then, after a moment of what looked like thought, it had given back the worm. Folded.

She was not thinking about the worm.

"I am *excellent* at this," she said. "I was the best angler in the novitiate."

"Were there other anglers in the novitiate?"

"There was a pond."

"So, no."

"Brother Hollis fished once."

"And?"

"He fell in."

Sage laughed. Low, lazy, from the flat rock above the stream where she'd been lying for an hour like a cat someone had paid to be there. Her hair was a black spill over the stone. One arm was folded behind her head, which pulled her collar open, which showed the ink.

Rule six: don't look at the tattoo.

Ymir had made rule six on day four, when she'd caught herself following the ink flames up Sage's throat to where they licked the corner of her jaw, and then wondering, with what she told herself was purely scholarly interest, where they *started*. Rules one through five were also about Sage. Rule two was *don't let her pick the campsite*, because Sage picked campsites the way other people picked fights.

She looked at the fish instead. The fish looked back.

Two weeks. Two weeks since the last road had given up and turned into a deer path, and the deer path had given up and turned into this. The forest was thick in a way forests weren't supposed to be. The light came down green and slow, like it had to push through something. The air hummed if she held her breath. At night the moss glowed faintly wherever she'd stepped, so every morning she could see exactly where she'd gone to pee, which was information she hadn't wanted.

And the creatures.

She had read all fourteen volumes of Brother Aldous's *Complete Bestiary of the Known Kingdoms*. Twice. Under a blanket, after bells, because the Mothers said reading after bells was a kind of vanity and Ymir had decided early that she could live with one vice if it was a quiet one. She knew the wyrms of the southern marsh. She knew the salt-hares. She knew the difference between a barrow-cat and a common cat, which was mostly attitude.

None of it was here.

Yesterday at dawn a stag had come down to the water with antlers made of glass. When it lowered its head to drink, they chimed. Ymir had dropped the kettle. The night before, a bird had sung the same song she'd been humming at supper, backwards. There were lights under the river that moved against the current. There was something that had followed them for a whole afternoon, always one tree behind, and when Ymir finally spun around with her hand on her sword, all she'd found was a small grey animal holding a pinecone the way a person holds a hat.

It had nodded at her.

She had nodded back. She still didn't know if that had been the right thing to do.

"You're doing it again," Sage said.

"Doing what?"

"Remembering the stag. You go all soft around the mouth."

"I do not."

"It had *glass antlers*," Sage said, in a voice that was very clearly supposed to be Ymir's. It was too high and too breathless and it was also, horribly, accurate.

"It did have glass antlers!"

"I know. I was there."

"You didn't even look at it."

That was the thing. That was the thing Ymir kept circling back to at night, lying on her bedroll pretending to pray. Every time the forest did something impossible, Ymir would gasp and grab Sage's arm and point, and Sage would turn. But not toward the thing.

Toward her.

"I've seen a stag before," Sage said.

"With glass antlers?"

"No." Sage turned her head on the stone. Her eyes were very blue, the deep kind, like a well you'd be told not to lean over. "But I'd never seen you see one. That was new."

Ymir's face went hot all at once, like someone had opened an oven door in her head.

*That's not fair,* she thought. *You can't just say things like that and then lie there.*

Nobody looked at Ymir like that. People *liked* Ymir. Everyone liked Ymir. The Mothers liked her, the kitchen boys liked her, the other paladins liked her in the easy way you like weather that's reliably nice. She smiled and people smiled back. She polished her armor and prayed at sunrise and was helpful, *so* helpful, and it all went down smooth, like warm milk.

Nobody had ever watched her like she was the strange animal in the forest.

She wanted it. That was the problem. She wanted it the way you want to press on a bruise.

"You're bothering my fish," she said.

"Your fish is bothering itself." Sage stretched, all spine. "Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to anyway."

"Why'd they send a paladin?"

Ymir kept her eyes on the water.

"The north has hunters," Sage went on. "The duke has soldiers. Pretty good ones, I hear. Mean. And the king sends a blessed knight of the Dawn, alone, to kill one animal. That's either a very special animal or a very special knight."

"The duke asked for one."

"Mm. And you were chosen."

"I was."

"By whom?"

"...By the Council."

"Just like that."

The sword at Ymir's hip went faintly warm. Not hot. Just warm, the way a cheek goes warm. It did that when she got too close to the edge of something.

"I raised my hand," Ymir said, "while they were choosing."

Sage sat up.

Slowly. Delighted. Ymir could feel the delight from where she was crouched, it came off Sage like heat off a road.

"You can't lie," Sage said.

"The Sworn of the Dawn don't lie."

"That's not the same as *can't*."

"It's very close to can't."

"What happens? Do you go blind? Does your god smite you?"

"The sword gets warm."

"Warm."

"Like it's embarrassed for me."

Sage laughed so hard she had to lie back down. Ymir watched the fish, and the fish watched her, and she thought, not for the first time, that she had been happier alone.

That was a lie too. Luckily, she hadn't said it out loud.

"All right," Sage said, when she could. "So. What's in the capital that a woman who can't lie is running from?"

The forest went on humming. Somewhere upstream, the glass stag chimed.

Ymir thought of the lemons.

The first basket had come in midwinter, when lemons cost more than her armor. Everyone in the capital knew what lemons in winter meant. Her mother had cried and held her face and said *the second princess, Ymir, the second princess*, and the king had clapped her on the shoulder and called it an honor to the whole Order. The second basket came a week later. The third came with a note, in a small neat hand. It said *I can wait*.

Ymir had not eaten a lemon since. She had started leaving rooms that smelled of them.

"I'm fishing," she said.

The sword stayed cool. It was true. She was.

Sage looked at her for a long moment. Ymir waited for the next jab, the next *mm*. It didn't come.

"Fish, then," Sage said quietly.

The line jerked.

Ymir yelped and hauled and the bootlace held, gods bless the cobbler, and the fish came up out of the water in a bright arc and landed flapping on the bank. It was the one that had been staring at her. She was almost sure. It had the same expression.

Something dark was caught in its gills. A thread, thin as a hair, black and oddly stiff. Ymir picked it free with two fingers. It was cold. Colder than the water. She flicked it back into the stream and wiped her hand on her trousers without really thinking about it.

"Congratulations," Sage said. "You've caught the stupidest fish in the forest."

"I've caught the *only* fish in the forest. You didn't catch anything."

"I didn't try."

"Then you don't get any."

Sage slid down off the rock and crouched beside her. Close. Closer than she needed to be to look at a fish. Her shoulder was almost against Ymir's arm, and Ymir could smell smoke on her, always smoke, as if Sage walked around with a small fire tucked somewhere nobody could see. The inked flames were right there. Rule six was right there.

"Sharing is a virtue, paladin," Sage said. "Isn't it one of yours?"

"It's number four."

"What's number one?"

"Honesty."

Sage smiled. Not the sharp smile. A slower one.

"Then tell me honestly," she said, "that you don't want me here."

Ymir opened her mouth.

At her hip, before she had said a single word, the sword began to warm.

"Clean your own fish," Ymir said.

Sage laughed, and didn't move away.
